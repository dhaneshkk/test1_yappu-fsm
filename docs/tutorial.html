An interactive tutorial for yappu-fsm

How a 2,000-year-old meter teaches machines to stay in line

Tamil prosody turns poetry into a finite-state machine. This tutorial builds that machine one rule at a time, uses it to steer a language model, and then applies the same idea to patient charts: NegEx scopes decide what is negated, coding rules written as plain English sentences run as Datalog, and a trie makes invented ICD-10-CM codes impossible.

Chapter 01 · Mathirai

Every letter has a duration

Tamil meter is quantitative. A short letter (குறில், kuril) lasts 1 mathirai, a long letter (நெடில், nedil) lasts 2, and a dotted consonant (ஒற்று, otru) lasts ½. One mathirai is traditionally the time of a blink or a finger snap.

Beat meter

Chapter 02 · Asai

Letters pair up into Ner and Nirai

Letters group into metrical syllables called asai. The rules are short enough to fit on a card:

  1. A long letter always closes its own Ner.
  2. A short letter followed by a short or long letter joins it in one Nirai.
  3. A short letter on its own is a Ner.
  4. Dotted consonants attach to whatever came before.

Write Ner as 1 and Nirai as 0, and every word becomes a short binary string. Rule 2 is the one most often got wrong: கூடினால் is கூ/டினால் (10), not three syllables.

Chapter 03 · Seer

Binary strings name the metrical feet

A metrical foot (சீர்) is a word read as its asai string. With one, two or three asai there are 2, 4 and 8 possibilities, and each has a name (vaipadu) that is itself a word with that rhythm: தேமா (Thema) is 11, புளிமா (Pulima) is 01. A Venba may only use the two-asai feet and the four three-asai feet ending in Ner; the four ending in Nirai (Kanicheer) are forbidden.

Chapter 04 · Thalai

A three-state machine decides which word may come next

Thalai (தளை, binding) is the rule linking the end of one foot to the start of the next. For a Venba it reduces to a tiny state machine:

  • after a Maa foot (ends in Ner), the next foot must start with Nirai (Iyarseer Vendalai);
  • after a Vilam foot (ends in Nirai), it must start with Ner (Iyarseer Vendalai);
  • after a Kay foot (three asai ending in Ner), it must start with Ner (Venseer Vendalai).

Anything else is a different meter's linking and breaks the conversational rhythm (Seppalosai) of the Venba.

Step through a poem

Chapter 05 · Nerisai Venba

Fifteen feet, one blueprint, one validator

A Nerisai Venba has four lines of 4, 4, 4 and 3 feet. The fourth foot of line 2 is the Thanichol, set off by a dash, and it must rhyme with lines 1 and 2. The final foot (Eetrucheer) is a single asai (Naal, Malar) or one followed by a short final u on a hard consonant (Kaasu, Pirappu), as in கொக்கு. Every one of the 14 links must be Vendalai.

    Chapter 06 · Ethukai and Monai

    Rhyme on the second letter, alliteration on the first

    Ethukai (எதுகை) compares line-opening words on their second letter: the second letters share a consonant and the first letters have the same length. That is why அடக்க and மடைத்தலையில் rhyme (ட / டை), while an exact-letter check would wrongly reject them.

    Monai (மோனை) is alliteration on the first letter, with traditional equivalence groups (Ina Ezhuthu): vowels அ ஆ ஐ ஔ, இ ஈ எ ஏ, உ ஊ ஒ ஓ, and the consonant pairs ச/த, ஞ/ந, ம/வ. In the decoder it is a soft bonus, never a hard rule.

    Chapter 07 · Constrained decoding

    Give the model only words that keep the poem valid

    A language model scores every possible next token. A logits processor can set a score to −∞ before sampling, which removes that option. VenbaLogitsProcessor removes every option that would break Vendalai, the rhyme, or the final-foot rule. It also looks ahead and removes words that would leave no way to finish the poem, so generation never dead-ends.

    Below, you play the model: pick any green word. The grey ones are what the processor masks, with the reason. The word list is the package's sample lexicon.

    You are the language model

    The real processor does this at the level of bytes, so it also works when a model's tokens split a Tamil letter in half. The next chapter shows why that matters.
    Chapter 08 · Bytes and tokens

    Why the checks run on whole words, not tokens

    Models do not see letters. They see tokens, and for Tamil a token is often a fragment: a consonant without its vowel sign, or a lone pulli. The asai of a fragment means nothing. So each vocabulary token is mapped to its raw UTF-8 bytes, and the processor walks a byte trie of whole allowed words. A token is allowed if appending its bytes keeps the text on a path that can still be completed.

    Chapter 10 · NegEx and ConText

    How far does “no” reach?

    The linker follows NegEx (Chapman et al.) and its ConText extension. Triggers come in four kinds:

    kinddirectionexampleseffect
    Pre-triggerforward, ≤ 6 tokensdenies, no, without, ruled out, absence of, no history ofnegates what follows
    Post-triggerbackward, ≤ 6 tokenswas ruled out, not demonstrated, never developed, absent, cannot be ruled outnegates or makes uncertain what came before
    Pseudo-triggernoneno change, gram negative, not ruled outstops a false negation
    Terminatornoneaside from, secondary to, due to, except, plus clause wallsends a scope early
    ConTextforwardhistory of · mother had · return if, if the patient developshistorical · family history · hypothetical

    Scope explorer

    A fixed window is the method's known weakness: a term nine words from its trigger is missed, and in “Probable CAP with cough and fever” the symptoms fall inside the scope of “probable”. Dependency-based scoping (NegBio) is the next step.
    Chapter 11 · Coding rules as sentences

    Write the guideline in English, run it as Datalog

    Some coding conventions relate one fact to another: diabetes with CKD becomes E11.22 plus a stage code, hypertension with CKD becomes I12.9 instead of I10, and Excludes1 pairs must never appear together. These are written as controlled-English sentences in the style of Attempto Controlled English, parsed once, and compiled to Datalog with stratified negation. Datalog always terminates.

    Chapter 12 · Speed at scale
    
  
    From a full scan to a hash index
    
  
    
    
    The first linker compared every phrase in a note with every lexicon entry, so its cost grew in step with the lexicon. Real ICD-10-CM has about 74,000 codes. The linker now looks up candidates by each entry's first word: the exact word, its phonetic skeleton, and every one-letter deletion. The deletions catch a single typo, insertion or swap. A test checks that the index returns the same findings as the full scan on the gold notes and 300 random typos.

    Chapter 15 · Run it yourself
    
  
    One Colab cell, one CLI, one endpoint
    
  
    
    
    Colab. Open notebooks/yappu_fsm_colab_single_cell.ipynb in Colab and run its only cell. The whole package is embedded in it: it writes the files, runs the test suite, validates a Venba, codes a note, runs constrained decoding through model.generate(), and starts the endpoint. Switch CODING_SETTING to "inpatient" at the top to apply Guideline II.H.

    Constrain any Hugging Face model
    

    from transformers import LogitsProcessorList
from yappu_fsm.decoding import ICD10LogitsProcessor
allowed = linker.allowed_codes(linker.process(note))
proc = ICD10LogitsProcessor(tokenizer, allowed)
out = model.generate(**tokenizer(f"Note: {note}\nCODES:", return_tensors="pt"),
                     logits_processor=LogitsProcessorList([proc]), max_new_tokens=40)
    
  
    
  
    What the processors guarantee: every completed output is in the allowed language. A Venba always validates, and every code comes from the linker's allowed set without repeats. They do not make a code clinically right; a certified coder reviews the output.
